8.1.1 2025–2027 考纲统一 15 大全球议题 × 三视角 (Global / National / Local) 破题速查表

2025–2027 最新考纲重大整合：Component 1 笔试、Component 2 (IR) 与 Component 3 (TP) 现已彻底统一共享同一套 15 个全球议题。下表逐一列出官方英文原名，并拆解每个议题如何无缝跨越 Global（国际准则/跨国系统）↔ National（国家税制/立法/财政取舍）↔ Local（社区/校园/家庭切身冲突） 三层视角，供 IR 选题、TP 找痛点与 C1 笔试背景激活一站式速查：

# 官方议题原名 & 中文 核心研究切入点 (A* 争辩焦点) Global 视角 National 视角 Local 视角与核心冲突
01 Arts in society
社会中的艺术		 公共财政缩紧时，国家是否应削减艺术馆与非遗补贴以优先保障 STEM 与基建？ UNESCO 倡导非遗保护与文化多样性作为人类共同财富。 财政部需平衡文化软实力输出与医疗、基础教育预算缺口。 地方剧团与社区画室靠补贴维持生计，家长却普遍施压学校多排理科课时。
02 Change in culture and communities
文化与社区变迁		 城市化与全球流行文化在多大程度上加速了方言与地方传统的消亡？ 跨国媒体与通用语降低跨文化交流门槛、扩大青年机遇。 国家语言政策在标准化办公语言与少数族群语言保护间摇摆。 乡村长辈担忧宗族节庆与母语断代，年轻一代却向往都市现代生活方式。
03 Climate change, energy and resources
气候变化、能源与资源		 高收入国家是否应为发展中工业国承担绿色转型资金，而非单方面征收碳关税？ IPCC 呼吁各国立即退煤以守住 1.5°C 全球温升安全防线。 发展中国家把廉价煤炭电力视为工业脱贫与能源主权的刚需。 矿区家庭忧虑煤矿关停导致失业，沿海低洼村庄却已饱受咸潮与洪涝冲击。
04 Conflict and peace
冲突与和平		 全面经济制裁能否有效制止武装冲突，还是反而对平民造成不成比例的人道伤害？ 联合国与外交联盟主张以非武力经济施压维护国际法准则。 受制裁国政府将制裁塑造为外部威胁并收紧国内资源配给。 当地医院遭遇进口药品短缺与恶性通胀，普通家庭沦为最直接受害者。
05 Development, trade and aid
发展、贸易与援助		 在长期脱贫效果上，直接基础设施投资是否优于紧急人道物资援助？ 多边发展银行推动长期造血项目与公平贸易准入机制。 受援国政府警惕附加政治条款的外援，倾向自主主权基建合作。 当地小农户常因免费进口粮食倾销而破产，质疑短期救济破坏本地市场。
06 Digital world
数字世界		 政府应强制实施青少年社交媒体年龄验证，还是优先推进校园数字素养教育？ 跨国科技平台推崇无国界数据互联与算法自由创新效率。 立法机构必须在保护未成年人心理健康与兼顾数字经济活力间取舍。 偏远乡村缺乏宽带设备（接入鸿沟），而城市家长焦虑算法成瘾与隐私泄露。
07 Education for all
全民教育		 生成式 AI 辅导工具能否真正缩小城乡教育差距，还是会进一步拉大不平等？ UNESCO 视普及优质基础教育为打破跨代贫困的核心人权。 教育部在有限财政下难以同时升级农村硬件与高薪留住偏远师资。 乡村家庭受限于网费、停电与农忙用工需求，学生即便有账号也难以稳定上线。
08 Employment
就业		 面对自动化与零工经济扩张，全民基本收入 (UBI) 或强力技能再培训哪项更可行？ ILO 警告跨境供应链压低劳工保障，呼吁全球最低体面劳动标准。 国家担心过度管制零工平台与重税补贴会削弱企业投资竞争力。 外卖骑手依赖弹性接单维持月薪，却面对无医保与算法罚单的日常困境。
09 Environment, pollution and conservation
环境、污染与生态保护		 沿海地区应划定严厉海洋禁渔保护区，还是补贴低影响传统渔业实现折中转型？ 国际生物多样性公约推动 2030 年保护 30% 海洋生态恢复鱼群。 渔业部门兼顾水产出口创汇与远洋生态执法巡逻的高昂成本。 世居小渔村面临生计断流与补偿不到位危机，校园则聚焦一次性塑料减量。
10 Globalisation
全球化		 跨国制造业外包究竟提升了新兴经济体工资，还是加剧了“逐底竞争”？ WTO 框架依托比较优势压低消费品价格并促进跨境资本流动。 发达国家面对本土产业空心化焦虑，新兴国则争抢外资建厂红利。 工业小镇居民一边享受就业机会，一边承受河水污染与高强度加班压力。
11 Health and wellbeing
健康与福祉		 遏制青少年肥胖与超加工食品危害，应征收糖税还是彻底改革校园供餐标准？ WHO 倡导公共卫生优先于商业游说，主张高糖高脂产品警示标签。 财政部门权衡食品制造业税收、就业岗位与长期医保支出攀升压力。 低收入社区缺乏平价生鲜超市（食物荒漠），校园食堂依赖高利润瓶装饮料创收。
12 Law and criminality
法律与犯罪		 面对青少年初犯，修复性司法与社区矫治是否比强制最低刑期更能降低再犯率？ 联合国少年司法准则强调教育重塑与减少监禁带来的标签化效应。 司法预算紧缺使得狱政扩建与社区社工跟踪长期存在资源竞争。 经历偷盗的社区商户出于安全焦虑呼吁重判威慑，社工却指出贫困家庭诱因。
13 Media and communication
媒体与传播		 社交媒体平台应对算法放大选举虚假信息负直接法律责任吗？ 跨国信息战无视国界，国际学界讨论平台算法透明度强制审计。 政府需严防假借“打击谣言”为由实施过度审查并挤压公民独立新闻空间。 地方传统纸媒倒闭使基层社区失去核实市政新闻的本地记者，谣言在群聊滋生。
14 Migration and urbanisation
移民与城市化		 高技能医护与工程人才跨国流失，给母国带来“人才枯竭”还是“侨汇反哺”？ 老龄化发达国家通过快速签证吸收全球护理与 STEM 人才填补缺口。 输出国财政出资培养了大学毕业生，却无法提供匹配国际水平的薪资留人。 输出国乡村诊所遭遇医生荒，特大城市城中村外来务工者则面对高房租与户籍壁垒。
15 Political power and action
政治权力与公民行动		 将法定投票年龄降至 16 岁或实行强制投票，能否真正解决青年政治冷漠？ 民主治理研究关注低青年投票率如何削弱长期代际政策合法性。 执政体系在扩大选民基数与担忧年轻群体易受民粹短视频操纵间拉锯。 高中生质疑“如果市政厅从不回应校园安全提案，投一张选票又有何意义”。

Source 1 — Global Connectivity Monitor (GCM), Annual Digital Equity Report 2025

International Briefing According to data published in January 2025, 67% of the global population now uses the internet regularly, yet penetration falls to just 29% across rural districts in low-income nations. Within secondary education, 84% of urban schools report reliable high-speed broadband, compared with only 31% of schools in remote agricultural provinces. Between 2020 and 2025, average mobile data tariffs declined by 18% worldwide; nevertheless, low-income rural households still spend up to 9.4% of monthly income on a basic 2 GB mobile plan—nearly five times above the United Nations affordability benchmark of 2%.

GCM researchers warn that by 2030, cloud-based examination platforms and artificial intelligence tutoring tools will widen academic achievement gaps between connected and disconnected regions by at least 25% unless rural bandwidth expands rapidly. Universal digital access should be treated as a fundamental human right rather than a commercial luxury. [Methodological Note: Figures are compiled from self-reported national telecom audits across 42 participating countries and exclude off-grid nomadic settlements.]

Source 2 — Field Interview Excerpts: Voices from a Remote Mountain District, Northern Province (2025)

Teacher · Ms Lin Mei (Science teacher for 14 years at a 280-pupil rural boarding school): “People in cities think uploading online assignments solves everything, yet many of my pupils walk three kilometres uphill after sunset just to catch a faint 3G signal. Half our families share one ageing feature phone among four relatives, and our grid electricity cuts out three or four evenings every week. Last year a charity donated twenty laptops, but within eight months nine screens cracked or batteries failed with no technician within eighty kilometres to repair them.”

Student · Karan, aged 16 (Lives in a farming village 18 km from town): “During harvest months I help my parents until dusk, so I cannot join afternoon live video lessons. An offline revision tablet charged by a small solar panel would help me study past papers at night without wasting money on mobile data. Still, I worry that pre-loaded quizzes cannot answer my chemistry questions when I get stuck.”

Source 3 — Corporate White Paper: AeroBeam Satellites Inc., “SkyLink Education Initiative”, March 2025

Written by Dr Marcus Vance (PhD in Telecommunications Engineering; Vice President of Public Partnerships at AeroBeam Satellites Inc.): “Satellite broadband is the only rapid, future-proof cure for rural educational inequality. Unlike slow fibre cables that take a decade to lay across mountain ridges, our low-Earth-orbit classroom dish installs in two hours. During our six-month pilot across 12 selected rural schools, average weekly online quiz completion jumped by 38%, proving that high-speed connectivity immediately transforms student outcomes.”

“Under our SkyLink Education partnership, regional ministries receive a 40% introductory discount on satellite hardware terminals for Year 1, allowing budgets to deliver instant digital equity. Waiting for local library buildings is outdated bureaucracy when space-based internet already brings world-class teachers into every classroom.” [Footnote: Standard commercial subscription tariffs apply from Year 2 onward; the 12 pilot schools were chosen for having paved road access and backup diesel generators.]

Source 4 — Policy Forum Debate: How Should Governments Narrow the Rural Digital Divide?

Writer A · Prof. Amara Okafor (Director of Public Infrastructure Policy, Independent Education Research Institute): “Rather than locking school budgets into costly recurring satellite contracts or handing out fragile personal gadgets, regional authorities should invest in solar-powered Community Digital Hubs inside rural towns and central schools. A 2024 OECD/UNESCO comparative audit of 90 rural districts found that shared community centres lowered five-year per-pupil operating costs by 52% and kept 78% of desktop equipment functional after four years—compared with only 34% survival for individual tablet giveaways. Crucially, staffed hubs provide trained local facilitators who maintain hardware and guide pupils through difficult concepts. Admittedly, students living in distant hamlets face travel barriers after school hours; however, pairing hubs with staggered weekend shuttle routes and extended evening lighting resolves access equitably without draining public budgets.”

Writer B · Elena Rostova (Founder of ‘ConnectEveryChild’ Advocacy Group & Former EdTech Marketing Director): “Waiting years for government committees to construct concrete hubs is a moral betrayal of an entire rural generation! Last summer I visited a mountain village where three children literally cried with joy when handed solar-charged tablets pre-loaded with offline maths games—nothing beats owning your own personal screen. Everyone knows individual devices triple test scores overnight because pupils learn independently at their own pace from home. Public computer rooms inevitably turn into locked, dusty closets managed by indifferent officials. Governments should immediately distribute low-cost offline tablets to every rural pupil or pay satellite firms to wire remote rooftops without delay.”

1. 展开 Question 1 (14 marks) 完整 Mark Scheme 与逐小题英文满分示范

Q1(a) · 2 marks Award 1 mark per accurate statistic from Source 1 showing access disparity (max 2):

  • Global internet penetration is 67%, whereas rural districts in low-income nations stand at only 29% (1m).
  • 84% of urban secondary schools have reliable broadband compared with 31% in remote agricultural provinces (1m).
  • Low-income rural households spend up to 9.4% of monthly income for 2 GB data vs the UN 2% affordability benchmark (1m).

Q1(b) · 4 marks 1 mark for accurate identification + 1 mark for category explanation (× 2):

  • Prediction: “By 2030, cloud-based learning platforms and AI tutoring tools will widen academic achievement gaps... by at least 25%.” (1m) — This makes a projection about a future outcome that has not yet occurred and cannot currently be verified as fact (1m).
  • Value Judgement: “Universal digital access should be treated as a fundamental human right rather than a commercial luxury.” (1m) — This expresses an ethical/moral stance about what society ought to prioritise (should), grounded in values rather than objective data (1m).

Q1(c) · 4 marks 2 marks for two barriers from Source 2 + 1 mark strength + 1 mark limitation:

  • Barriers (2m): (i) Poor connectivity/shared hardware — pupils walk 3 km uphill for weak 3G and share one feature phone among four family members (1m); (ii) Unreliable electricity & zero local maintenance — grid blackouts occur 3–4 evenings weekly and 9 of 20 donated laptops broke within 8 months with no repair technician within 80 km (1m).
  • Strength (1m): High experiential relevance/authenticity — Ms Lin Mei has 14 years of first-hand rural teaching experience and Karan provides direct student perspective on harvest-time schedule conflicts.
  • Limitation (1m): Small, non-representative sample — draws on only two voices from a single mountain district, so findings cannot safely be generalised to all rural regions nationally.

Q1(d) · 4 marks Explains multi-step causal chain connecting economic & infrastructural barriers across local/global scales (Level 2 = 3–4m):

Model Answer: Locally, low family incomes mean rural households spend up to 9.4% of monthly earnings on basic mobile data (Source 1) and must share one ageing phone while teens undertake farm labour during harvests (Source 2). Without reliable grid power or repair technicians nearby, even donated laptops quickly fail (Source 2). Globally, as urban and wealthier school systems adopt cloud-based platforms and AI tutors (84% urban broadband vs 31% rural, Source 1), rural pupils fall further behind in examination readiness, limiting higher-education and formal employment prospects and locking disconnected communities into intergenerational economic disadvantage.

2. 展开 Question 2A (8 marks) & Question 2B (8 marks) Level 4 Mark Scheme + 英文完整高分范文

Q2(a) Level 4 · 7–8 marks Must evaluate both strengths and weaknesses explicitly with specific evidence from Source 3 (RAVEN: Expertise, Recency, Evidence vs Vested Interest, Cherry-picked Sample, Omission, Tone).

Q2(a) Model Answer (8/8):
Source 3 possesses notable strengths as evidence, yet its reliability is severely compromised by commercial vested interest and selective sampling. On the positive side, the author, Dr Marcus Vance, holds a PhD in Telecommunications Engineering, lending technical expertise regarding satellite installation speed (two hours versus years for mountain fibre). The source is also highly recent (March 2025) and cites a concrete quantitative pilot outcome—a 38% increase in weekly online quiz completion across 12 rural schools—alongside a specific Year 1 40% hardware discount.

However, major weaknesses undermine its credibility. First, as Vice President of AeroBeam Satellites Inc., Dr Vance has an undeniable commercial vested interest: his firm directly profits from securing multi-year government contracts, and the footnote admits standard commercial subscription fees apply from Year 2 onward, omitting long-term recurring costs. Second, the 12 pilot schools represent a tiny, cherry-picked non-random sample chosen specifically for paved roads and backup diesel generators—conditions absent in truly remote districts like Ms Lin Mei’s (Source 2). Third, measuring “weekly quiz completion” does not prove higher final exam attainment, and his absolutist language (“the only rapid, future-proof cure”, “outdated bureaucracy”) shows clear promotional bias. Overall, Source 3 is partially useful for technical installation claims but weak as neutral policy evidence.

Q2(b) Level 4 · 7–8 marks Must specify research methods (triangulation), sample size/control comparison, measurable comparison indicators, and methodological limitations.

Q2(b) Model Answer (8/8):
To test whether high-speed satellite broadband raises rural exam performance more effectively than offline resources, I would design a one-academic-year comparative quasi-experimental study combining quantitative attainment data with qualitative interviews. First, I would select a stratified sample of 20 rural secondary schools matched by baseline exam scores, household income, and electricity reliability, randomly assigning 10 schools to receive satellite broadband and 10 comparable schools to receive solar-powered tablets pre-loaded with identical offline curriculum materials.

Second, to isolate academic impact rather than merely quiz clicks, all Year 10–11 pupils (around 1,500 learners) would sit a standardised pre-test in Maths and Science in Week 1 and an externally marked mock examination after 36 weeks, comparing mean percentage-score gains between the two groups. Third, I would conduct semi-structured interviews with 30 teachers and 40 pupils across both arms to examine practical barriers such as power cuts, repair delays, and lack of live feedback.

Finally, a key limitation of this plan is confounding teacher-quality variation and seasonal harvest absenteeism, which could skew test scores independently of technology; additionally, interviewees might give socially desirable praise if they fear losing free equipment, requiring anonymous survey triangulation.

3. 展开 Question 3 (16 marks) Level 4 Mark Scheme + 英文完整逐维对比满分范文

Q3 Level 4 · 13–16 marks Point-by-point comparative evaluation of both arguments (evidence quality, reasoning/assumptions, balance/concessions, tone/vested interest) + clear supported judgement on which is more convincing.

Q3 Model Essay (16/16):
Overall, Professor Amara Okafor’s argument (Writer A) is significantly more convincing than Elena Rostova’s (Writer B) because Okafor substantiates her case with independent longitudinal evidence, anticipates counter-arguments, and maintains an objective tone, whereas Rostova relies on emotive anecdotes, sweeping unverified claims, and unstated assumptions.

First, in terms of evidence quality and authority, Okafor cites a credible, independent 2024 OECD/UNESCO comparative audit spanning 90 rural districts, providing concrete metrics showing shared digital hubs cut five-year per-pupil costs by 52% and retain 78% equipment functionality versus 34% for individual handouts. In stark contrast, Rostova offers zero verifiable statistical studies. She grounds her position in a single emotionally charged personal anecdote (“three children literally cried with joy”) and an unsubstantiated generalisation (“Everyone knows individual devices triple test scores overnight”), which constitutes an empirical overstatement.

Second, regarding balance and logical reasoning, Okafor constructs a clear causal link connecting shared hubs with trained facilitators who solve hardware maintenance—a real barrier echoed in Source 2. Crucially, she demonstrates balanced reasoning by explicitly conceding a major drawback—that pupils in distant hamlets face after-school commute barriers—before proposing practical mitigation via weekend shuttles and extended lighting. Conversely, Rostova presents a strictly one-sided dismissal of public centres (“locked, dusty closets”) and relies on the unstated assumption that rural teenagers can master complex curricula autonomously on offline screens without trained instructors or repair support.

Finally, tone and potential bias sharpen this contrast. Okafor adopts measured academic language appropriate for her independent policy role, whereas Rostova employs alarmist rhetoric (“moral betrayal of an entire generation”) and exhibits potential ideological or commercial bias given her background as a former EdTech marketing director and advocacy founder. Therefore, despite minor logistical hurdles in Okafor’s hub model, her argument is far more rigorously reasoned and persuasive.

4. 展开 Question 4 (24 marks) Level 4/5 Mark Scheme + 英文完整四段政策决策满分范文

Q4 Level 4/5 · 19–24 marks Clear recommendation + sustained comparison against all other options using multi-dimensional criteria (cost sustainability, longevity/maintenance, equity/access, pedagogical support) + explicit acknowledgement and mitigation of chosen option’s drawbacks + integrated evidence across Sources 1–4.

Q4 Model Essay (23–24/24):
To narrow the rural educational digital divide over a five-year horizon, the regional government should prioritise Option 2: building solar-powered public Community Digital Hubs with trained staff. Evaluating all three strategies across financial sustainability, hardware longevity, and pedagogical equity reveals that Option 2 outperforms both commercial satellite subsidies and mass tablet giveaways, provided its geographical commute limitations are actively mitigated.

Option 2 offers the strongest long-term value for money and operational durability. As demonstrated in Source 4, the 2024 OECD/UNESCO audit across 90 rural districts shows shared centres reduce five-year per-pupil operating costs by 52% while keeping 78% of equipment functional after four years. Incorporating solar power directly addresses the frequent grid blackouts reported by Ms Lin Mei in Source 2, while employing trained facilitators eliminates the acute maintenance crisis where donated laptops broke within months with no technician for eighty kilometres. Furthermore, staffed centres preserve human instructional guidance, overcoming Karan’s concern (Source 2) that automated tools cannot answer difficult science questions.

By contrast, Option 1 (subsidising private satellite contracts) is financially unsustainable and narrow in impact. Although Source 3 claims satellite dishes install in two hours and lifted quiz completion by 38%, its Year 1 40% discount conceals steep commercial subscription fees from Year 2 onward, threatening to drain public education budgets indefinitely. Moreover, satellite connections alone fail when households lack reliable electricity or repairable student devices (Source 2). Similarly, Option 3 (distributing pre-loaded solar tablets) offers immediate household portability for harvest-working students like Karan, yet fails on durability and educational depth. Source 4 indicates that individual device giveaways suffer a 66% failure rate within four years (only 34% survival), making nationwide replacement prohibitively expensive, while offline static content still leaves disadvantaged pupils behind as global curricula shift toward interactive assessment (Source 1).

Admittedly, Option 2 has two notable drawbacks: high upfront capital construction time and travel barriers for pupils in isolated villages like Karan’s (18 km away), particularly during evening hours or harvest peaks. However, the ministry can mitigate both weaknesses by converting existing rural school libraries or town halls into solar hubs immediately rather than constructing new buildings, pairing centres with subsidised weekend bus shuttles, and keeping a small lending pool of offline solar tablets at hubs specifically for remote agricultural seasons. By combining Option 2’s durable infrastructure with targeted Option 3 support, the government delivers equitable, cost-effective progress over five years.
